Phishing simulation

This was a practice email. Nothing bad happened.

Attackers send messages like this every time a major airline outage makes the news. Take two minutes to see the signs, so the real one doesn't get you.

The email you just opened

1From: SkyBridge Airlines <no-reply@skybridgeairlines-status.example>

Subject: Important notice: Your upcoming travel may be affected

one or more flights associated with your account may be delayed — no flight number, no route, no confirmation code

2Check Flight Status  → hover shows skybridgeairlines-status.example/status/check

3📞 1-800-555-0192  (SkyBridge Customer Support)

  • 1
    The sender's domain has an extra word. SkyBridge's real address would end in skybridgeairlines.example. This one adds -status, making it look like a legitimate notifications subdomain. It's close enough to pass a quick glance — which is exactly the point.
    Check the full address, not just the display name. Any extra word or hyphen after the company name is a signal to pause.
  • 2
    The button goes to a lookalike domain. Hovering over "Check Flight Status" reveals the same fake domain — not the airline's real site. In a real attack this page would ask for your login or payment details.
    Hover before you click (or press and hold on a phone). If the URL isn't the company's known domain, go directly to their site by typing the address yourself.
  • 3
    The phone number connects to a scammer, not the airline. Attackers register numbers that ring to call centers staffed with people posing as airline agents. The script is polished: they'll look up your name, offer to rebook you, then ask for a rebooking fee by gift card or wire transfer — or request your account credentials to "pull up your reservation."
    Never call a number listed in an email. Find the real number on the airline's official website or the back of your credit card.

The detail this email left out — on purpose

Notice that the email never mentions a flight number, a route, a departure time, or a confirmation code. That's not an oversight — it's the design. Attackers send this to thousands of people at once. They don't know who has a flight booked or when. By staying vague, the message works on anyone who might have travel coming up and is already rattled by outage news.

Real airline emails always include your booking reference and flight details. An email that only says "your upcoming travel may be affected" without any of that information wasn't written for you — it was written to cause enough worry that you act before you think.

Why this email is timed for right now

Attackers watch the news. When a major airline outage is in the headlines, they know travelers are already expecting disruption emails — and are stressed enough to act without thinking. The same tactic appears around holiday travel weeks, big storms, and other events that put people on edge about their plans.

When disruption is already in the headlines, treat any unexpected travel email with extra skepticism, not less.

What to do next time

  1. Don't click the button and don't call the number. Use the Report Phish button in Outlook to send it to the security team.
  2. To check on a real flight, open a browser and type the airline's address yourself — don't follow links from email.
  3. If you already called the number and gave information, contact the IT Service Desk right away. Fast reporting makes it much easier to fix, and you won't be in trouble.

Phishing simulation

This was a practice email. Nothing bad happened.

Meeting reminders feel routine — and routine is exactly when people stop thinking. Take two minutes to see what made this one dangerous.

Step 1 — The email you opened

1From: TeamHub <notifications@teamhub-notify.example>

Subject: Reminder: Your meeting with Sarah Chen is in 2 days

2027 Project Discussion  ·  Sarah Chen  ·  Thu Nov 20  ·  10:00 AM

2Review Meeting Details  → hover shows teamhub-notify.example/m/details?id=8824af

  • 1
    The sender's domain has an extra word. TeamHub's real notifications would come from teamhub.example. This one uses teamhub-notify.example — an extra word that makes it look like a legitimate notifications subdomain. At a glance, in a busy inbox, it reads as real.
    Check the full address. Any extra word or hyphen after the company name is a signal to pause.
  • 2
    The button goes to the same fake domain. Hovering over "Review Meeting Details" shows the lookalike domain — not teamhub.example. The first click felt safe because the meeting details page looked right. That's the design: lull you into trusting the page before the real trap appears.
    Hover before you click (or press and hold on a phone). If the domain isn't exactly what you'd expect, don't click.

Step 2 — The meeting details page

The page you landed on looked like a real TeamHub meeting view — a title, an organizer, a date and time. It was designed to feel like confirmation that the meeting was legitimate. Here's what it looked like:

The lure page (picture only)
2027 Project Discussion
Organized by Sarah Chen  ·  Fernhollow Outfitters
Date: Thursday, November 20, 2026
Time: 10:00 AM – 10:30 AM
Location: TeamHub — online meeting
Meeting ID: 847 293 1056
Passcode: Wx4rP9
Join Meeting Reschedule
  • 3
    The Reschedule link on that page was the real trap. Hovering over it would have revealed the same fake domain — teamhub-notify.example — not TeamHub's real site. In a real attack, clicking it would take you to a page designed to steal your login credentials or install malware.
    Even when a page looks right, hover over every link before you click. The domain tells the truth.

The habit that stops this every time

Before clicking anything in a meeting-related email, ask yourself one question: is this meeting already on my calendar? Open your calendar app and check. If the meeting is real, it is already there — you do not need the email at all. If it isn't there, the email is not legitimate.

This attack worked by making you act before you thought. Meeting reminders feel routine, so we process them quickly. Slowing down — even for ten seconds — breaks the trick entirely.

What to do next time

  1. Don't click. Use the Report Phish button in Outlook to send the email to the security team.
  2. To verify a meeting, open your calendar app directly — never follow links from email.
  3. If you already entered credentials on a page like this, contact the IT Service Desk immediately. Fast reporting makes it much easier to fix, and you won't be in trouble.
